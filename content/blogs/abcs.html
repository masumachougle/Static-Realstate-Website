The following is an overview of some basic terms that you may encounter when purchasing or leasing a property:

Real Estate Listing / Market Value

The term "real estate listing" refers to properties listed for sale through a real estate agent. A typical real estate listing will have pictures of the property, a description of the property, an asking price, and information about the property.

The asking price is often derived from the market value of the property, which is calculated by considering previous recent sales in the area of similar properties, the current market demand for the property, the date of any recent sales of similar properties and the state of the real estate market.

Market value is a range of potential values for a given period and location and is subject to market conditions, time and physical state (condition).

Purchase Price and Appraisal

The purchase price is the price agreed to by the buyer and seller, and an appraisal is a valuation of the property ("appraisal" refers to the appraiser's report) conducted by an independent licensed appraiser on behalf of a lender.

The lender uses the appraisal to help determine whether or not the property's value supports the purchase price, which means that if a buyer and seller agree to a certain purchase price, but the assigned value of the property based on the appraisal does not support that amount, the buyer may have to renegotiate with the seller or bring additional funds to settlement.

Earnest Money & Down Payment

Earnest money is a deposit that a buyer gives to a seller, as a show of good faith, after both parties agree to the sale of a property.

The buyer may use the earnest money to help them complete the required down payment or closing costs; however, it is typically held in an escrow account until such time as either the buyer or seller has fulfilled their obligations under the given agreement.

The Purchase Agreement & Its Contingencies

Once both parties have successfully negotiated and agreed upon the terms of the sale, they will create a legally binding contract called a purchase agreement.

The purchase agreement includes important information, including the sale price, financing terms, deadlines for each of the buyers and sellers, and all of the agreed-upon contingencies — the conditions necessary to close on the property (for example, obtaining a mortgage, getting the results of a satisfactory home inspection and having the property appraised at or above the agreed-upon sale price).

In this way, contingencies provide both buyers and sellers with protection throughout the entire real estate process.

Home Inspection and Seller's Disclosure

Home Inspections give the buyer the knowledge of the condition of a property before purchases are made. The inspection process identifies major issues with systems/structures as well as potential repairs or inspection areas that may become necessary after the purchase.

The Seller's Disclosure Form identifies any known defects or issues with the property disclosed by the seller to the buyer. Both the Inspection and the Disclosure Form provide transparency that can lessen the chance of misunderstandings/disagreements and build trust between both parties.

Closing/Escrow/Title Costs

A Title is proof of ownership of a property. Prior to Closing, a Title Search will be conducted to ensure that there are no Liens, Delinquent Taxes, Disputes of Ownership, or other issues that could adversely affect the transaction.

The funds and documentation involved in a Real Estate Transaction are held in Escrow by an impartial third party to assure that all provisions and conditions of the transaction are fulfilled before ownership (Title) is transferred to the buyer.

Closing costs are fees involved in finalising the transaction. They may include Lender Fees, Title Insurance, Attorney Fees and Transfer Taxes. Depending on Local Customs and Negotiated Terms, these costs are usually split between the Buyer and Seller.

Equity and Closure

During the final stage of a real estate transaction, known as the closing, all transactions will take place. At this point, the seller will hand over ownership of the property to the buyer, and a series of documents will be signed, funds will be exchanged, and the title of the property will formally pass from one party to the other.

Upon completion of the transaction, the buyer will begin to build equity in the property, defined as the difference between the market value of the home and the outstanding balance on the mortgage note.

As the mortgage balance decreases through normal repayment of the loan, and the home appreciates in its overall value, the buyer will have an overall equity build-up in the property that can serve as an additional financial asset.

Conclusion

By familiarising oneself with the basic terminology of the industry, it will ease many of the stressors and misconceptions typically found during a real estate transaction. Once both parties know what the terms mean, they can communicate openly and honestly about their needs and expectations, identify opportunities for improvement, and feel confident moving forward in the transaction together.

An experienced real estate agent will explain all aspects of the transaction and guide his/her clients through it.
